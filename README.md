# Sapling

A physics and chemistry sandbox, built with [Fizmo](https://github.com/ThatNewCoderJSCpp/Fizmo)'s numerics and chemistry, [Grove](https://github.com/ThatNewCoderJSCpp/Grove) for the window and [Walnut](https://github.com/ThatNewCoderJSCpp/Walnut) for its script. It's the physics companion to Arbor: Arbor graphs equations, Sapling runs worlds.

The window has two sections, picked at the top.

**Physics** is being rebuilt. For now it has the stage, play, pause, step, reset and speed controls, and the settings and readouts panels, ready for whatever is running in it.

**Chemistry** has these tabs:
- **Bonding sandbox**: covalent, ionic, metallic and network bonding.
  - *Covalent*: pick nonmetals and metalloids (aluminum and beryllium too) from a periodic table and build molecules yourself. Click to place atoms, drag between atoms to bond them, and click a bond to make it double or triple. Fill H, Tidy (a VSEPR shape in 3D), Snap to correct (keeps your drawing but gives every bond its typical length and every atom its VSEPR angles, so water bends to 104.5°) and Fix Lewis help out, and polyatomic ions and common molecules load as templates. The side panel tracks the name (organic molecules get their IUPAC name from the structure: longest chain, branches such as ethyl and methyl, and numbering like 3-ethyl-2-methylhexane), stability verdict, Lewis structure (lone pairs, formal charges, broken octets, radicals), resonance and average bond orders, the shape around each atom, polarity and dipole, the intermolecular forces (London dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding with its donors and acceptors, ion–dipole), which is strongest and what that means for boiling and dissolving, and ΔH. Click a bond to see its order (whole or fractional), average length, bond energy, ΔH, ΔS and ΔG to break it at the chosen temperature and pressure, how many are broken, its polarity and its stretching frequency. Lone pairs, formal and partial charges, bond dipoles and the molecular dipole can be shown on the drawing. Any selected atom can be made a heavier or lighter isotope, which changes the molar mass and the stretching frequencies. A formula with one central atom (XeF4, SF6, PCl5) builds itself. Gases also get Cp, Cv, γ and the speed of sound, and molecules get their permittivity, refractive index and polarizability as a gas or liquid.
  - *Ionic*: pick a metal and a nonmetal (a metal with more than one common ion, like copper or iron, opens a row of its charges, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺, right next to it), pick any cation and anion from the ion lists, or type them. The crystal is built in its likely structure and assembles itself from scattered ions. The panel gives the lattice energy four ways with the Born–Haber cycle, radii, coordination, density, solubility, heat capacity, thermal conductivity, how well it conducts as a solid and when molten, its permittivity, refractive index, dielectric strength, stiffness, sound speeds, thermal expansion, hardness and magnetic susceptibility.
  - *Metallic*: pick up to eight metals, plus carbon, nitrogen, boron or hydrogen to sit between the metal atoms, and their proportions (the others adjust so they always add to 100%), or a preset (brass, bronze, steel, solder, gold). The panel names the alloy, says what each element does (the base metal, atoms that take the place of base-metal atoms, and small atoms tucked into the holes), gives the carbon grade of a steel and whether it's stainless, and checks the Hume-Rothery rules; the drawing shows the lattice with its electron sea, which drifts when an electric field is turned on. Heat, electricity and magnetism get their own sections: specific and molar heat capacity, Debye temperature, thermal conductivity and diffusivity, resistivity and its temperature coefficient, the Drude relaxation time, mean free path, mobility and Hall coefficient, the plasma frequency, the skin depth at any AC frequency, the magnetic ordering and susceptibility, the Fermi energy, work function, photoelectric threshold, Seebeck coefficient, reflectance, and the elastic moduli, sound speeds and thermal expansion.
  - *Network*: giant covalent structures in 3D: diamond, graphite, graphene, a carbon nanotube, fullerene C₆₀, three ranks of coal (a simplified model), silicon, silicon carbide and cubic and hexagonal boron nitride, with their bonding, hardness, conduction and uses, each atom's bonds and angles, and which form of carbon is stable at the chosen temperature and pressure. Carbon also goes into metals (steel) and ionic carbides (CaC₂, Al₄C₃).
  - Temperature and pressure (any units, each with a slider that follows the unit) change bond breaking, and melt or boil lattices past their melting and boiling points, which move with pressure (the boiling point a lot, the melting point a little).
  - **Save** writes the sandbox to `.sapling/chemistry/covalent`, `ionic` or `metallic` in the folder Sapling runs from (`SAPLING_SAVE_DIR` moves it; on Android it lives in the app's data folder). `.sapling/` is in `.gitignore`.
- **Ions & names**: 157 polyatomic ions you can search and filter, each with a 3D or Lewis structure, its other names and its acid. A namer turns formulas into names and names into formulas (ionic compounds, acids, hydrates, molecular compounds, organics, carbohydrates; condensed structures such as CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 get their IUPAC names with the longest chain, branches and numbers), and two ions combine into a compound.
- **Reactions**: predict the products from only the reactants (combustion, synthesis, decomposition, single and double replacement, neutralization, equilibria with ⇌), balance an equation with your choice of arrow, see ionic equations, redox changes and ΔH, ΔS, ΔG and K. Formula boxes draw subscripts, charges and arrows as you type (N_2, Cu^2+, ->), and the explanations show formulas the same way. Predictions and balanced equations show the heat released or absorbed. A stoichiometry solver takes masses, moles, solution volumes, liquid volumes, gas volumes, gases collected over water or partial pressures of any species, reactants or products, each as the amount at the start, used, left over or made, and works out everything left blank; each species' state (solid, liquid, gas or dissolved) is guessed and picks the units it offers. Anything it had to assume is shown in orange with the amount, and amounts that can't be solved say why in red. It gives the heat released or absorbed, the percent yield, each measured at its own temperature, pressure or volume if needed, the limiting reactant, every amount, and every gas before and after the reaction with its partial pressure, mole fraction and volume, in an open system or a closed container, with water and other vapors condensing when they should and an optional real-gas (van der Waals) correction.
- **Solvers**: over 80 equations (gases, solutions, acids and bases including pOH, pK_b and K_aK_b = K_w, thermochemistry, kinetics, electrochemistry, light, and electricity and materials: Ohm's law, wire resistance, capacitors, Coulomb's law in a material, Snell's law, skin depth, heat conduction, thermal expansion and the photoelectric effect). Fill in what you know in any units and solve for the rest. Also a unit converter, percent composition, and empirical and molecular formulas.
- **Kinetics**: zero-, first- and second-order rate laws with half-lives and the straight-line plots; mechanisms of several elementary steps (reversible ones too) integrated numerically; Arrhenius plots and k at two temperatures; reaction energy profiles with and without a catalyst; Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distributions with the fraction of molecules above Ea; and fitting your own concentration–time data to find the order and k.
- **Acids & bases**: the pH, pOH, pK_a, pK_b and every species of any mixture of acids, bases and salts (strong, weak and polyprotic), built by adding each one as a solution (molarity and volume), by moles or by mass, plus extra water, or typed as concentrations; titration curves for strong–strong, weak–strong, strong–weak and weak–weak pairs with an acid or a base in the flask, picked from lists of real acids and bases (polyatomic ions like carbonate and phosphate included), with every equivalence and half-equivalence point and an indicator; which form of an acid is present at each pH; and buffers with Henderson-Hasselbalch, buffer capacity and what adding acid or base does. Plots can show pOH instead of pH.
- **Spectra**: the emission (or absorption) lines of about 60 elements and any mixture of them, shown as the strip you'd see through a spectroscope, a plot, and the color of the light, with flame-test colors. It also works backward: type the lines you saw, paste a measured spectrum, or read a photo of a spectrum strip (with the wavelengths of its edges), and it estimates which elements are there and in what proportions; or match a color (picked, or averaged from a photo) to a mix of flame colors.
- **Phase diagrams**: from a substance name, formula, SMILES, element, anything saved in the bonding sandbox, or a molecule, ionic compound or metal built right there with the same tools as the sandbox. You get the 2D pressure–temperature diagram with a draggable state point, and the 3D pressure–specific volume–temperature surface. Click the triple point, critical point or a normal melting or boiling point for its details, drag along a line to follow it, and drag across the 3D surface to read points. The axes can be linear or logarithmic and set to any range from 0 K and 0 atm up, and a plasma and quantum-gas view shades where the gas is mostly ionized (Saha equation) and draws where an ideal gas of the particles would become a Bose-Einstein condensate or a degenerate Fermi gas. Missing enthalpies of vaporization are estimated, and every property can be edited to make your own diagram.
- **Radial probability**: P(r) for each subshell of any element or ion, with the total, cumulative curves, peaks and nodes. Showing one subshell zooms the distance axis to fit it.
- **Electron clouds**: any orbital or whole atom in 3D as dots (colored by the sign of ψ, one color, or by orbital type), as shaded "balloon" surfaces holding 90% of the probability, or both, and as a 2D slice drawn as a heat map, in grayscale, as contour lines, as dots, or as the 90% outline.
- **Elements**: a periodic table with each element's shells, orbital-box diagram, full and noble-gas electron configurations and properties. Extra neutrons pick an isotope (mass, abundance, half-life, decay, spin and binding energy), the nucleus can be one circle with the counts or every proton and neutron, flat or in 3D (the proton, neutron and electron counts are always shown), and heat, electricity, magnetism, permittivity, light, stiffness, sound and the free-electron properties are worked out at any temperature.
- **Units**: the units every result is shown in. Inputs with units have their own dropdowns.

## Building

Sapling needs CMake 3.18 or newer, a C++17 compiler, Ninja (recommended) and the same system libraries as Grove (X11, Xft, fontconfig, FreeType, HarfBuzz, OpenSSL, zlib, libjpeg and the Vulkan headers on Linux).

```
cmake -S . -B build -G Ninja -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
cmake --build build
ctest --test-dir build --output-on-failure
build/bin/sapling
```

CMake fetches Fizmo (`SAPLING_FIZMO_TAG`), Grove (`SAPLING_GROVE_TAG`) and Walnut (`SAPLING_WALNUT_TAG`). Sapling declares Fizmo itself, before Grove, so the Fizmo it asks for is the one Walnut and Grove build with too. It needs Fizmo's `numerics` and `chemistry` components (v0.3.5 or newer). To build against a local Fizmo checkout instead, pass `-DFETCHCONTENT_SOURCE_DIR_FIZMO=/path/to/Fizmo`.

| Option | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `SAPLING_BUILD_TESTS` | ON | builds `sapling_tests` |
| `SAPLING_BUNDLE` | ON | runs `sapling-host --bundle` to make `sapling` with its window and compiled script built in |
| `SAPLING_PACKAGE` | OFF | makes release packages with CPack |
| `SAPLING_WITH_WALNUT` | ON | builds Walnut alongside Grove for the script |

`sapling-host` runs the UI straight from the `UI` folder, which is handy while working on it; set `SAPLING_UI_DIR` to point it somewhere else.

## How it's put together

- `Include/sapling`, `Source`: the core library. `Simulation` is the base class for anything the sandbox runs (reset, step, draw, readouts and parameters). `Canvas` is a small anti-aliased software rasterizer the host draws each frame with: lines, shapes, polygons, shaded spheres and a stroke font with Greek letters, sub- and superscripts.
- `Include/sapling/chemistry.hpp`, `Source/chemistry`: the chemistry views (`ChemView`) the host draws into canvases. There's the bonding sandbox, structure, phase diagram, radial probability, electron cloud and element views, plus a small 3D renderer, plot axes, unit preferences, the JSON save files and `chemistry_tool` for the naming, balancing, prediction and solver panels. All the chemistry itself comes from Fizmo's `chemistry` component.
- `Tools/sapling.cpp`: the host program. It owns what's running, steps it on a timer and hands the pixels to Grove's `<canvas>`; with nothing running it draws an empty grid. It also routes mouse and keys to the chemistry canvases, and gives the scripts the `sapling.chem.*` functions.
- `UI`: the window (`sapling.grove`), its styles (`sapling.bark`, `chemistry.bark`), its scripts (`sapling.wal`, `chemistry.wal`) and one file per chemistry tab in `UI/chemistry`.
- `Tests/sapling_tests.cpp`: the canvas, the `Simulation` base class (stepping, parameters and their ranges, readouts, drawing), number formatting, and the chemistry views, tools, JSON and saving.

## Branches and releases

Work happens on `dev`, and `main` is fast-forwarded to it. Pushes to either branch run CI (`.github/workflows/ci.yml`). Pushing a `v*` tag runs `.github/workflows/release.yml`, which builds Linux (x86_64, arm64), macOS (arm64, x86_64), Android (one APK) and Windows (x86_64, i686, arm64 with llvm-mingw) packages and attaches them to the release. The tag has to match `VERSION` in `CMakeLists.txt`.

### macOS

`Sapling-<version>-macos-arm64.tar.gz` is for Apple Silicon Macs and `macos-x86_64` for Intel Macs (macOS 11 or newer). Everything Sapling needs that macOS doesn't come with (FreeType, HarfBuzz, fontconfig, libjpeg and what they use) is in the package's `lib` folder, so nothing else has to be installed. With `-DSAPLING_PACKAGE=ON` on macOS, the build copies those libraries out of Homebrew into `build/bin/lib` (`cmake/macos_bundle_libs.cmake`) and points the programs at them.

The download isn't signed by Apple, so macOS refuses to open it at first. Remove the download flag once, then run it:

```sh
xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine Sapling-*
./Sapling-*/sapling
```

### Android

`Sapling-<version>-android.apk` runs on phones, tablets and Chromebooks. Copy it to the phone and open it there (Android asks once to allow installing apps from that source), or install it over USB with `adb install -r Sapling-*-android.apk`. The release workflow's `.github/scripts/android-apk.sh` builds it: a desktop `grove` and `walnut`, then `libsapling_android.so` and Grove's Android kit for each ABI with the Android NDK, then `grove build --target android` compiles the script for the phone and makes the APK. Signing uses the `SAPLING_KEYSTORE_BASE64`, `SAPLING_KEYSTORE_PASS` and `SAPLING_KEY_ALIAS` secrets when they're set; without them each build gets a new debug key, so an older install has to be uninstalled first.
